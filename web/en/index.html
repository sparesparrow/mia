MANIFESTO OF DISOBEDIENCE

Don't ask the authorities to protect your privacy. Don't ask corporations to respect your ownership. One side promises safety, the other convenience, and you pay for both with your data.

Your car knows more about you than your mum does. How much you drive, where you park, how you brake. A new car sends that to the manufacturer and the insurer. MIA reads it for you alone.

Build your own infrastructure. A Raspberry Pi under the seat, a couple of ESP32s behind the dash and a phone in the mount. No cloud, no subscription. The hardware is yours, the code is open, your data stays under your own control, and nobody can switch it off remotely.

Read-only. Open. Yours. She only listens to the car. She sends nothing on the bus and writes nothing to the control units. MIA can't kill your engine or unlock your doors, even if she wanted to.

Under mass surveillance there is no other way. Your own tools, your own hardware, and no authority you have to trust. Not even us: the code is open, read it.

Join us. Or keep driving a car that snitches on you instead of to you.

DISOBEDIENCE: FREEDOM FROM BELOW

Nobody hands you freedom from above. Elections, petitions and new regulations won't give you your privacy back. Agorists call it counter-economics, the Czech dissident Václav Benda called it the parallel polis: instead of begging for better rules you build your own structures. Agorists want the old ones to starve. Benda, a Catholic philosopher in Charter 77, expected the official structures under that pressure to “either fall apart or usefully restore themselves”. MIA is one brick of them: a car that listens to you, not to an agency or a manufacturer.

Since 7 July 2024 every newly registered car in the EU must carry an event data recorder, a black box (Regulation (EU) 2019/2144, Art. 6). It only records a few seconds around a crash and the data is anonymised, but it cannot be switched off and nobody asks whether you want one. In his chapter on private roads, Urza imagines a driver who “wouldn't want a device in the car that keeps recording his position”: instead of a GPS beacon, the road owner offers him a chip that only the readers on toll streets can see. A market asks you; a law tells you. MIA reads your car's data for you alone and sends it to neither the manufacturer nor the insurer.
